# 📌 PART 1: INTRODUCTION TO AIR

## 🌬️ What is Air?

- Air is a **non-homogeneous mixture** of different gases in the atmosphere
- The composition of air refers to the **chemical composition of the troposphere**

---

## 🌍 The Troposphere:

- The **troposphere** is the **lowest layer of the atmosphere**
- Extends from the Earth's surface to about **12 km (39,000 ft) altitude**
- Contains about **80% of the mass** of the Earth's atmosphere
- **Nearly all of the atmosphere's water vapour** exists in this layer
- **Just three gases account for 99% of dry air:**
  1. **Nitrogen (N₂)**
  2. **Oxygen (O₂)**
  3. **Argon (Ar)**

---

## 📊 Composition of Air (Percentage by Volume):

| Gas | Chemical Formula | Percentage (%) |
|---|---|---|
| **Nitrogen** | N₂ | **78.084** |
| **Oxygen** | O₂ | **20.946** |

| **Neon** | Ne | 0.001818 |
| **Helium** | He | 0.000524 |
| **Methane** | CH₄ | 0.000187 |
| **Water Vapour** | H₂O | 0% to 5% (averaging ~0.4%) |

# 📌 PART 2: AIR POLLUTION

## Definition:

> "Air pollution means the **presence in the atmosphere of one or more air contaminants** in such quantities and of such duration as **is or tends to be injurious to human health or welfare, animal or plant life, or property**, or would unreasonably interfere with the enjoyment of life or property."

### Extended Definition:
> "Presence in the air (outdoor atmosphere) of **one or more contaminants or combinations thereof** in such quantities and of such durations as may be or tend to be **injurious to human, animal or plant life, or property**, or which unreasonably interferes with the comfortable enjoyment of life or property or conduct of business."

---

## Forms of Air Pollutants:

- Air pollutants come in the form of:
  - **Gases**
  - Finely divided **solid aerosols**
  - Finely divided **liquid aerosols**
- **Aerosols** = "any solid or liquid particles suspended in the air"

---

## Air Pollutant – Key Facts:

- A pollutant can be **solid** (large or sub-molecular), **liquid**, or **gas**
- It may originate from a **natural** or **anthropogenic** (human-made) source (or both)
- Anthropogenic sources have changed the composition of global air by **less than 0.01%**

### Examples of Natural Air Pollution:
- Ash
- Salt particles
- Pollen and spores
- Smoke
- Windblown dust

---

# 📌 PART 3: SOURCES AND CLASSIFICATION OF AIR POLLUTANTS

Air pollutants can be classified in many ways:

---

## Classification 1: According to Origin

### A. Primary Pollutants:
- Pollutants that are **emitted directly from identifiable sources**
- Produced by:
  - **Natural events** (e.g., dust storms and volcanic eruptions)
  - **Human activities** (e.g., emissions from vehicles, industries)
- **Examples:** Smoke, dust, oxides of sulphur and nitrogen, hydrocarbons, particulate matter

### B. Secondary Pollutants:
- Pollutants that are **formed in the atmosphere** by chemical interactions between:
  - **Primary pollutants** AND
  - **Atmospheric constituents**
- They are **not directly emitted** but formed through reactions
- **Examples:** Sulphur trioxide, ozone, ketones, sulphuric acid, nitric acid, carbonic acid

> 💡 **Simple Understanding:** Primary = directly released into air; Secondary = formed in the air by reactions

---

## Classification 2: According to State of Matter

### A. Gaseous Air Pollutants:
- Exist in a **gaseous state** at normal temperature and pressure
- Examples: **Carbon dioxide, Nitrogen dioxide, Sulphur oxides**

### B. Particulate Air Pollutants:
- **Not gaseous** substances
- They are **suspended droplets, solid particles, or mixtures** of the two
- Examples: dust, soot, smoke, fumes

---

## Classification 3: According to Sources

### A. Natural Sources:
- Volcanic eruptions
- Deflation of sand and dust
- Forest or wild fires of natural vegetation
- Sulphur springs
- Natural geysers
- Organic and inorganic decays
- Vegetative decays
- Marsh gases
- Cosmic dust
- Pollen grains of flowers
- Photochemical reactions
- Soil debris
- Wild animals in their natural habitat (decomposition gases, waste)

### B. Man-Made (Anthropogenic) Sources:
- Industries and factories
- Urban centres
- Aircraft
- Nuclear experiments
- Automobiles
- Agriculture
- Domestic burning of wood
- Burning of fossil fuels
- Deforestation
- Mining
- Waste treatment plants
- Power plants

---

## Classification 4: According to Location of Emission Source

### A. Stationary Sources:
- An emission source that **does not move**
- Examples: power plants, chemical and manufacturing industries
- Often defined as **large emitters** who release relatively consistent quantities of pollutants

#### i. Point Source:
- **Industrial and non-industrial stationary equipment** or processes considered significant sources of air pollution
- A facility is considered to have **significant emissions** if it emits **one ton or more in a calendar year**

#### ii. Area Source:
- **Many smaller stationary sources** located together
- Individual emissions may be **low** but **collective emissions can be significant**
- Typically emit **less than 25 tons per year** of any combination of hazardous air pollutants, or **less than 10 tons per year** of any single hazardous air pollutant
- **Examples of area sources:**
  - Locomotives operating within a railyard
  - Multiple flue gas stacks within a single industrial plant
  - Open burning and forest fires
  - Evaporation losses from large spills of volatile liquids
  - Piping leaks, industrial wastewater treatment ponds, rock and quarry operations, tank farms

---

## Factors that Affect Air Pollution:

| Factor | Explanation |
|---|---|
| **Emissions** | Traffic, industrial, and domestic emissions are major contributors |
| **Geography (Terrain)** | Mountains and valleys can trap pollutants in certain areas |
| **Weather conditions** | Rain washes away pollutants; winds disperse them; humidity affects reactions |
| **Season** | Winter inversions can trap pollution; summer increases photochemical smog |
| **Time of day** | Morning and evening rush hours have higher traffic emissions |
| **Population density** | More people = more emissions, vehicles, and industrial activity |
| **Indoor vs Outdoor** | Indoor air can be more polluted than outdoor due to confined spaces |

# 📌 PART 4: NATIONAL AMBIENT AIR QUALITY STANDARDS (NAAQS)

## What is Ambient Air Quality?

- **Ambient air quality** = the **condition or quality of the outdoor air**
- **NAAQS** = Standards for ambient air quality with reference to various identified pollutants
- These are **notified by the CPCB** (Central Pollution Control Board) under the **Air (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Act, 1981**

---

## Major Objectives of NAAQS:

**(i)** To indicate **necessary air quality levels** and appropriate margins required to ensure the **protection of vegetation, health, and property**

**(ii)** To provide a **uniform yardstick** for the **assessment of air quality at the national level**

**(iii)** To **indicate the extent and need** of the monitoring programme

---

# 📌 PART 5: AIR QUALITY INDEX (AQI)

## Background:

- AQI was launched by the **Prime Minister in April, 2015**
- Initially started with **14 cities**, now extended to **71 cities in 17 states**

---

## What is AQI?

- AQI is a tool for the **effective communication of air quality status to people**
- It transforms **complex air quality data** of various pollutants into:
  - A **single number** (index value)
  - **Nomenclature** (category name)
  - **Colour** (colour coding)

---

## How AQI Works:

- AQI sub-index and health breakpoints are evolved for **eight pollutants:**
  - **PM₁₀, PM₂.₅, NO₂, SO₂, CO, O₃, NH₃, and Lead (Pb)**
- For which **short-term (up to 24-hours)** National Ambient Air Quality Standards are prescribed
- Based on measured ambient concentrations, a **sub-index is calculated** as a linear function of concentration
- **The worst sub-index determines the overall AQI**

### Example:
- Sub-index for PM₂.₅ will be:
  - **51** at concentration **31 μg/m³**
  - **100** at concentration **60 μg/m³**
  - **75** at concentration **45 μg/m³**

---

## AQI Categories and Health Breakpoints:

| AQI Range | Category | Associated Health Impacts |
|---|---|---|
| **0–50** | **Good** | Minimal Impact |
| **51–100** | **Satisfactory** | May cause **minor breathing discomfort** to sensitive people |
| **101–200** | **Moderate** | May cause **breathing discomfort** to people with lung disease such as asthma and discomfort to people with heart disease, children, and older adults |
| **201–300** | **Poor** | May cause **breathing discomfort to people on prolonged exposure** and discomfort to people with heart disease with short exposure |
| **301–400** | **Very Poor** | May cause **respiratory illness** to people on prolonged exposure; effect may be more pronounced in people with lung and heart diseases |
| **401–500** | **Severe** | May cause **respiratory effects even on healthy people** and serious health impacts on people with lung/heart diseases. Health impacts may be experienced even during light physical activity |

> 💡 There are **SIX AQI categories:** Good, Satisfactory, Moderately Polluted, Poor, Very Poor, and Severe.

# 📌 PART 6: EFFECTS OF AIR POLLUTION ON HUMAN HEALTH

## Health Effects of Various Air Pollutants:

### 1. 🚬 Tobacco Smoke
- Generates a **wide range of harmful chemicals**
- **Major cause of cancer**
- Affects even **passive smokers** (persons in vicinity who are not smokers themselves)
- Effects range from:
  - Burning sensation in eyes or nose
  - Throat irritation
  - Bronchitis
  - Severe asthma
  - Decrease in lung function
  - Cancer

---

### 2. 🦠 Biological Pollutants
- Mostly **allergens**
- Can cause:
  - **Asthma**
  - **Hay fever**
  - Other **allergic diseases**

---

### 3. 🧪 Volatile Organic Compounds (VOCs)
- Can cause **irritation of eyes, nose, and throat**
- In **severe cases:**
  - Headaches
  - Nausea
  - Loss of coordination
- **Long-term exposure** → suspected damage to **liver and other body parts**

---

### 4. 🧫 Formaldehyde
- Exposure causes **irritation to eyes and nose**
- May cause **allergies** in some people

---

### 5. 🔩 Lead (Pb)
- **Prolonged exposure** causes:
  - Damage to the **nervous system**
  - **Digestive problems**
  - In some cases, **cancer**
- **Especially hazardous to small children**

---

### 6. ☢️ Radon
- A **radioactive gas** that can **accumulate inside houses**
- **Originates from rocks and soil** under the house
- Its level is dominated by **outdoor air** and other gases emitted indoors
- Exposure increases the **risk of lung cancer**

---

### 7. 🟡 Ozone (O₃) – Ground Level
- Makes our **eyes itch and burn**
- Associated with **increase in respiratory disorders** such as asthma
- **Lowers our resistance** to colds and pneumonia

---

### 8. 🟠 Oxides of Nitrogen (NOₓ)
- Can make **children susceptible to respiratory diseases** in winters
- Contributes to smog formation

---

### 9. ⚫ Carbon Monoxide (CO)
- **CO combines with hemoglobin** → lessens the amount of oxygen entering blood through lungs
- This binding with proteins causes **changes in function** of affected organs:
  - **Brain**
  - **Cardiovascular system**
  - **Developing fetus**
- Effects:
  - **Impairs concentration**
  - **Slows reflexes**
  - Makes us **confused and sleepy**

---

### 10. 🟤 Sulphur Dioxide (SO₂)
- In air due to **combustion of fossil fuels**
- Can **oxidize and form sulphuric acid mist**
- Leads to **diseases of the lung** and other lung disorders:
  - **Wheezing**
  - **Shortness of breath**
- Long-term effects difficult to ascertain as SO₂ exposure is often combined with SPM

---

### 11. 🔘 Suspended Particulate Matter (SPM)
- Consists of: **dust, fumes, mist, and smoke**
- Main chemical component of concern: **Lead (Pb)**
- Other components: **Nickel, Arsenic, diesel exhaust particles**
- When breathed in → **lodge in lung tissues** → cause:
  - **Lung damage**
  - **Respiratory problems**

### Why SPM is a Major Pollutant (Special Emphasis):
- **a)** Affects **more people globally** than any other pollutant on a continuing basis
- **b)** There is **more monitoring data available** on this than any other pollutant
- **c)** More **epidemiological evidence** has been collected on exposure to this than to any other pollutant

# 📌 PART 7: ECONOMIC EFFECTS OF AIR POLLUTION

## Overview:

- Air pollution affects **human health, agriculture**, and leads to a range of other impacts
- These impacts are projected to become **much more severe** in coming decades
- Rising emissions are projected to lead to **higher concentrations of PM₂.₅ and ground-level ozone**
- In several regions, PM₂.₅ and ozone concentrations are **already above WHO guidelines**

---

## Economic Statistics:

| Parameter | Current Value | Projected (2060) |
|---|---|---|
| Global economic costs | — | **1% of global GDP** |
| Healthcare costs | USD 21 billion (2015) | **USD 176 billion** |
| Lost working days | ~1.2 billion/year | **3.7 billion/year** |
| Premature deaths | ~3 million (2010) | **6–9 million annually** |
| Global welfare costs (premature deaths) | USD 3 trillion (2015) | **USD 18–25 trillion** |
| Welfare costs (pain and suffering from illness) | USD 300 billion (2015) | **USD 2.2 trillion** |

> 💡 **Key Point:** A large number of deaths occur in densely populated regions with high concentrations of PM₂.₅ and ozone — especially **China and India**, and in regions with aging populations such as **China and Eastern Europe**.

---

## Effects on Materials and Property (Economic Damage):

### A. Building Materials:
- Corroded and disfigured by air pollutants
- Smoke and aerosols adhere to stone, brick, and building surfaces → produce **unsightly coatings**
- **SO₂ and SO₃** in the presence of moisture react with **limestone (CaCO₃)**:
  - Forms **calcium sulphate (CaSO₄)** and **gypsum (CaSO₄·2H₂O)**
  - Acid converts limestone into **water-soluble bicarbonate** → leached away
  - Staining requires **additional cleaning expense**

### B. Paints:
- Areas of high pollution require **more frequent painting**
- Air pollutants **damage the protective coating** → expose underlying surface to attack

### C. Textiles:
- **Sulphur oxides** cause **deterioration** of natural and some synthetic textile fibres
- Most common permanent damage: **deterioration of nylon hose**

### D. Rubber:
- Main affected areas: **side walls of tyres** and forms of **electrical insulation**
- This damage is caused by **ozone**

### E. Leather:
- **Sulphur dioxide** causes leather to **lose much of its strength** and ultimately **disintegrate**

### F. Paper:
- Metallic impurities in modern paper accelerate the **conversion of absorbed SO₂ to sulphuric acid** in the presence of moisture

### G. Glass and Ceramics:
- Long exposure causes **change in surface appearance**
- Moisture and atmospheric pollution by acidic substances play a **major role in surface degradation**

### H. Electronics Industry:
- Air pollutants cause **thin insulating film to develop on contacts** → results in **open circuits and malfunctioning** of equipment

### I. Vegetation:
- Damage to vegetation is a **very important aspect of economic loss** due to air pollution

### J. Intangible Loss:
- Damage to **art objects and heritage monuments** throughout the world
- Examples of affected heritage sites:
  - **Taj Mahal (Agra, India)**
  - **Sri Channakeshava Temple (Belur, India)**
  - **Parthenon (Athens, Greece)**

---

## Policy Implication:
- **Policies to limit air pollution emissions** would:
  - Improve air quality
  - Reduce risks of very severe health impacts
  - Generate considerable **climate co-benefits**
- Other effects: Corrosion of metals, soiling and eroding building surfaces, fading of dyed materials, rubber cracking, spoiling of vegetation, effects on animals, interference with production and services

# 📌 PART 8: CONTROL OF AIR POLLUTION BY EQUIPMENT

## What are Air Pollution Control Devices?

- **Air pollution control devices** = A series of devices that work to **prevent a variety of different pollutants** (both gaseous and solid) from entering the atmosphere — primarily from **industrial smokestacks**

### Two Broad Categories:
1. **Devices that control PARTICULATE MATTER** escaping into the environment
2. **Devices that control ACIDIC GAS EMISSIONS**

> Note: Extraction methods differ for each specific type of pollutant; although complex, these devices have proven effective — overall emission levels have dropped with their implementation.

---

## A. Control of Particulate Matter

Specific machinery is used to remove particulate matter from flue gases. Physical means of separation are used (since particulate matter is large enough to be "caught" physically).

### Methods:

### 1. 🔌 Electrostatic Precipitators (ESP)

**Definition:**
- A type of filter that uses **static electricity** to remove soot and ash from exhaust fumes before they exit the smokestacks

**Working Principle:**
- Unburned particles of carbon in smoke are **pulled out of the smoke by static electricity**
- **Negative electrodes:** metal wires
- **Positive electrodes:** large plates
- Charged particles are attracted to oppositely charged plates
- Leaves clean, hot air to escape the smokestacks

**Why Important:**
- Vital to remove unreacted carbon from smoke — it can **damage buildings** and **harm human health** (especially respiratory health)

---

### 2. 🌀 Cyclone Separators

**Definition:**
- A separation device that uses the **principle of inertia** to remove particulate matter from flue gases

**Working Principle:**
- Dirty flue gas enters a chamber containing a **vortex (similar to a tornado)**
- Due to the difference in **inertia** between gas particles and larger particulate matter:
  - **Gas particles move UP** the cylinder
  - **Larger particles hit the inside wall and DROP DOWN**
- This separates particulate matter from flue gas
- Result: **Cleaned flue gas exits** from the top

---

### 3. 🧵 Fabric Filters

**Definition:**
- One fairly simple method to remove **dust from flue gases**

**Working Principle:**
- Fabric (generally **felt** — not woven cloth, as woven cloth allows dust through) is placed so that flue gases must **pass through it** before exiting the smokestacks
- When gas passes through, **dust particles are trapped in the cloth**
- In some cases, can also remove **acidic gases** if they utilize basic compounds

---

## B. Gas Control

- **More intense chemical methods** are required to separate polluting gases from flue gas
- Important because many **acidic gases in flue gas contribute to acid rain**

### Methods:

### 1. 🧼 Scrubbers

**Definition:**
- A type of system used to **remove harmful materials from industrial exhaust gases** before they are released into the environment
- When used specifically to remove **SOx (sulphur oxides)** → referred to as **"Flue Gas Desulfurization"**

**Two Types of Scrubbers:**

| Type | Working |
|---|---|
| **Wet Scrubbers** | Removal of harmful components of exhausted flue gases by **spraying a liquid substance** through the gas |
| **Dry Scrubbers** | Removal of harmful components of exhausted flue gases by **introducing a solid substance** (generally in powdered form) to the gas |

**Purpose:**
- By removing acidic gases from the exhaust before it is released into the sky → **scrubbers help prevent the formation of acid rain**

---

### 2. 🔥 Incineration

**Definition:**
- Used to **convert VOC emissions into carbon dioxide and water** through combustion

**Working Principle:**
- Incineration takes place in a specialized piece of equipment known as an **afterburner**
- Afterburner is built to create conditions necessary for **complete combustion:**
  - Sufficient burn time
  - High temperature
- The incinerated gas must be **mixed** to ensure complete combustion

**Types of Incinerators:**
- Bubbling bed technology
- Circulating bed technology (Fluidized-bed incinerator)

---

### 3. 🌍 Carbon Capture

**Definition:**
- **Carbon capture and storage (CCS)** = Process of capturing CO₂ and storing it below ground

**Working Principle:**
- Carbon dioxide is captured and stored:
  - **Underground** (pumped into geologic layers)
  - In **forests and oceans**
- Prevents CO₂ from entering the atmosphere

**Current Status:**
- This process is **rarely being used**
- But is talked about extensively as a way to **limit greenhouse gas emissions** leading to climate change

---

# 📌 PART 9: SMOKE AND ITS CONTROL

## What is Smoke?

- Smoke is a **collection of tiny solid, liquid, and gas particles**
- It is a type of **aerosol** released during **combustion of materials**
- Main components: carbon (soot), ash, volatile organic compounds

## Control of Smoke:

- Smoke control is achieved through the **air pollution control devices** mentioned above (ESP, Cyclone separators, Scrubbers, Fabric Filters)
- Additional measures:
  - Using **cleaner fuels** (natural gas instead of coal)
  - **Better combustion technology** to ensure complete burning
  - **Catalytic converters** in vehicles
  - **Regular inspection** and maintenance of industrial sources

# 📌 PART 10: OZONE DEPLETION

## What is the Ozone Layer?

- **Ozone layer** is like an **umbrella** located approximately **24 km (15 miles) from Earth's surface**
- It is an **essential component of the stratosphere**
- **Function:** Absorbs **short wavelength ultraviolet (UV) radiation** from the sun → heats the gases of the stratosphere in the process
- **This is why temperatures RISE with increasing altitude** in the stratosphere
- The ozone layer is the reason **life was able to move out of the oceans** and onto land → evolving into the diverse biosphere we know today

---

## Ozone Layer – Key Facts:

| Fact | Data |
|---|---|
| **World Ozone Day** | Celebrated on **September 16** every year |
| **Unit of measurement** | **Dobson Units (DU)** — named after G.M.B. Dobson who pioneered the study |
| **1 Dobson Unit** | = 0.01 mm thickness of stratospheric ozone |
| **Average ozone thickness** | **300 DU** |
| **Ozone Hole threshold** | When it falls below **200 DU** |
| **Ozone thickness variation** | **Thinnest around equator**, **thickest near poles** |

---

## Causes of Ozone Depletion:

### Primary Cause:
- Release of **Chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs)** and other **Ozone-Depleting Substances (ODS)**
- CFCs were used widely as:
  - **Refrigerants**
  - **Insulating foams**
  - **Solvents**

### Mechanism of Ozone Destruction:

**Step 1:** Although CFCs are **heavier than air**, they are eventually carried into the stratosphere — a process that can take **2 to 5 years**

**Step 2:** When CFCs reach the stratosphere, **UV radiation from the sun** causes them to **break apart**

**Step 3:** This releases **chlorine atoms**

**Step 4:** Chlorine atoms **react with ozone (O₃)** → starting **chemical cycles of ozone destruction**

> 💡 **Key Fact:** **One chlorine atom can break apart more than 1,00,000 (100,000) ozone molecules!**

---

## Effects of Ozone Depletion:

### Health Effects on Humans (Due to increased UV reaching Earth's surface):

| Health Effect | Explanation |
|---|---|
| **Cataract** | UV radiation damages the lens of the eye |
| **Genetic mutation** | UV rays can damage DNA |
| **Constriction of blood vessels** | UV exposure affects circulatory system |
| **Leukemia** | Blood cancer linked to UV radiation |
| **Breast cancer** | Linked to UV exposure |
| **Skin cancer (Basal and Squamous cell carcinomas)** | Strongly linked to **UVB exposure** |
| **Malignant Melanoma** | Less common but **far more dangerous** — lethal in about **15–20% of cases diagnosed** |
| **Snow Blindness (Photo Keratosis)** | Inflammation of cornea (outer coating of eyeball) |
| **Reduced resistance to infections** | UV suppresses immune system |

### Environmental Effects:
- **Reduced crop yield** — UV damages agricultural crops
- **Damage to aquaculture** — affects marine organisms and food chains
- Increased **tropospheric ozone** (a health risk at ground level)
- Increased **Vitamin D synthesis capacity** in sunlight (can be both positive and negative)

> 💡 **Important Note:** The above impacts are **NOT due to direct exposure to ozone** but due to the **UV rays that reach Earth's surface through the ozone holes**

---

## Control Measures for Ozone Depletion:

### Historical Timeline:

| Year | Event |
|---|---|
| **1979** | Ozone hole over Antarctica was first spotted |
| **1957–1985** | 18-year study showed **35% decrease** in ozone column over Antarctica |
| **1978** | Use of **CFC propellants in spray cans banned in the US** |
| **1980s** | Antarctic ozone hole appeared; CFCs more strongly linked to ozone depletion |
| **1987** | **Montreal Protocol** was signed — signatory nations committed to reduction in use of CFCs and other ODS |
| **1995** | CFC production banned in **developed countries** after 1995 |
| **Later** | Ban extended to **developing countries** |
| **Today** | **Over 160 countries** have signed the treaty |
| **2000** | International agreement (signed by **139 nations**) banning CFC production |
| **~2050** | Ozone layer expected to return to **normal levels** if we stop producing ODS |

### Montreal Protocol (1987):
- An **international agreement**
- Originally signed by **139 nations**
- Today **over 160 countries** have signed
- Committed nations to **reduce use of CFCs** and other ozone-depleting substances
- Amended to **ban CFC production after 1995** in developed countries

### Recovery Forecast:
- We **cannot make enough ozone** to replace what's been destroyed
- But if we **stop producing ozone-depleting substances**, natural ozone production reactions should **return the ozone layer to normal levels by about 2050**
- Delays in ending production could result in **additional damage** and **prolong recovery**

### Replacement Chemicals:
- Control mechanism stresses on **replacement of banned CFCs** by:
  - **Ammonia**
  - **Steam**
  - **Helium**
  - And other non-ozone-depleting alternatives

# 📌 PART 11: PHOTOCHEMICAL CHANGES

## What are Photochemical Changes?

- Photochemical changes occur when **sunlight reacts with pollutants** in the atmosphere
- UV radiation initiates **chemical reactions** between pollutants
- This is how many **secondary pollutants** are formed

---

## Photochemical Smog:

### Definition:
- A type of air pollution that results from chemical reactions of sunlight with hydrocarbons and nitrogen oxides released by automobiles and industrial sources

### Key Reactions:
- **NO₂** (nitrogen dioxide) + sunlight → **NO** (nitric oxide) + **O** (atomic oxygen)
- Atomic oxygen reacts with O₂ → forms **O₃ (ozone)** at ground level
- Ground-level ozone + hydrocarbons → forms **PAN (Peroxyacetyl Nitrate)** and other harmful compounds

### Effects of Photochemical Smog:
- **Eye and throat irritation**
- **Respiratory problems**
- Reduced visibility (**haze**)
- Damage to **vegetation and crops**
- Aggravation of **asthma and lung diseases**

---

## Photochemical Changes in Ozone Depletion:

- In the stratosphere, UV radiation causes **photolysis of CFCs** → releases chlorine
- **Cl + O₃ → ClO + O₂** (chlorine monoxide + oxygen)
- **ClO + O → Cl + O₂** (chlorine released again)
- This catalytic cycle **destroys ozone continuously**
- One chlorine atom can destroy **100,000 ozone molecules** through this photochemical cycle

---

## Summary of Photochemical Effects:

| Location | Process | Result |
|---|---|---|
| **Troposphere** (ground level) | Sunlight + NOₓ + VOCs → | Photochemical smog, ground-level ozone (harmful) |
| **Stratosphere** | UV + CFCs → | Ozone depletion (harmful) |
