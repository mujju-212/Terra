## 1.1 CONCEPT OF GLOBAL WARMING

### What is Global Warming?

Global warming is the gradual increase in the average temperature of the Earth's atmosphere and oceans. To understand this properly, let us trace the complete journey of sunlight on Earth:

**Step 1 — Sunlight reaches Earth:**
When sunlight (solar radiation) reaches the Earth, not all of it is absorbed.

**Step 2 — Reflection:**
About **30% of sunlight** is sent back into space by:
- Clouds
- Atmospheric particles
- Reflective ground surfaces (like snow, ice)
- Surface of oceans

**Step 3 — Absorption:**
The remaining **70% of sunlight** is absorbed by:
- Oceans
- Air (atmosphere)
- Land surfaces

This absorbed energy **heats up the surface of the planet and the atmosphere**, making life possible on Earth.

**Step 4 — Re-radiation:**
As the Earth warms up, it radiates this absorbed solar energy back into space in the form of:
- Thermal radiation
- Infrared rays

This process of radiating heat out into space naturally **cools the Earth**.

**Step 5 — The Greenhouse Effect (Natural):**
However, some of this outgoing radiation (thermal/infrared) is **re-absorbed** by certain gases present in the atmosphere, such as:
- Carbon dioxide (CO₂)
- Water vapours (H₂O)
- Ozone (O₃)
- Methane (CH₄)
- Other trace gases

These gases **re-radiate** the heat back to the surface of the Earth. These gases are called **Greenhouse Gases (GHGs)** because of their **heat-trapping capacity** — similar to how a greenhouse traps heat to keep plants warm.

> ✅ **Note:** The natural greenhouse effect is actually GOOD and ESSENTIAL for life. Without greenhouse gases, the Earth's average surface temperature would be extremely cold (around -18°C instead of the current +15°C). Life as we know it would not be possible.

### When Does the Problem Begin?

The problem started when **human activities artificially increased** the concentration of greenhouse gases in the atmosphere at an **alarming rate** over the past two centuries (since the Industrial Revolution).

- As of **2004**, over **8 billion tons of carbon dioxide** was being pumped into the atmosphere annually.
- This increased concentration of GHGs **traps more outgoing thermal radiation**, preventing it from escaping into space.
- This leads to the phenomenon known as the **"Human-Enhanced Global Warming Effect"** or **"Enhanced Greenhouse Effect."**

**Conclusion:** Recent scientific observations have confirmed that it is indeed the **human-enhanced greenhouse effect** that is causing the planet to heat up. The planet has experienced the **largest increase in surface temperature over the last 100 years**.

## 1.2 INDICATORS OF GLOBAL WARMING

Indicators of global warming are measurable signs or evidence that clearly show the Earth is warming. The following 10 indicators are recognized:

### 1. Air Surface Temperature
- The most direct indicator of global warming.
- Global average air surface temperatures have been rising steadily.
- Records show a consistent upward trend in temperature over the past century.
- Even a rise of 1–2°C in average temperature has profound consequences on climate systems.

### 2. Specific Humidity
- Specific humidity refers to the amount of water vapour present in the air.
- As temperatures rise, **more water evaporates** from oceans, lakes, and land surfaces.
- This increases the amount of water vapour in the atmosphere.
- Since water vapour is itself a greenhouse gas, this creates a **positive feedback loop** — more warming leads to more evaporation, which leads to even more warming.
- Increased specific humidity is a clear indicator that the atmosphere is warming.

### 3. Glaciers
- Glaciers around the world (Himalayas, Alps, Andes, Arctic, Antarctic) are **melting and retreating** at unprecedented rates.
- The rate of glacier retreat has accelerated significantly in the last few decades.
- Melting glaciers contribute to **rising sea levels** and threaten freshwater supplies for billions of people downstream.
- The shrinking of glaciers is one of the most **visible and dramatic** indicators of global warming.

### 4. Snow Cover
- The **extent and duration of snow cover** in the Northern Hemisphere has been decreasing.
- Snow is melting earlier in spring and arriving later in autumn.
- Reduced snow cover means less sunlight is reflected back into space (reduced albedo effect), causing the Earth to absorb more heat — another positive feedback loop.
- Changes in snow cover patterns disrupt ecosystems, agriculture, and water availability.

### 5. Temperature over Land
- Land areas are warming **faster than ocean areas**.
- This is because land has a lower heat capacity than water, meaning it heats up and cools down faster.
- Temperature records over land consistently show warming trends.
- Regions like the Arctic are experiencing warming at **twice the global average rate**.

### 6. Sea Surface Temperature (SST)
- Oceans absorb a significant amount of heat from solar radiation and the atmosphere.
- **Sea surface temperatures** have been rising steadily.
- Warmer sea surfaces fuel stronger storms and hurricanes.
- Higher SST leads to **coral bleaching** — one of the most visible ecological impacts of warming.
- SST is also closely linked to patterns like El Niño and La Niña.

### 7. Sea Ice
- **Arctic sea ice** extent and thickness have been declining dramatically.
- The Arctic has been losing sea ice at a rate of about 13% per decade.
- Less sea ice means more dark ocean water is exposed, which absorbs more heat (reduced albedo), further accelerating warming.
- **Antarctic sea ice** also shows variability and seasonal changes related to warming.
- Reduction in sea ice threatens the habitat and survival of species like polar bears and seals.

### 8. Sea Level
- Global **sea levels are rising** due to two main reasons:
  - **Thermal expansion** — as water warms, it expands in volume.
  - **Melting of glaciers and ice caps** — adding more water to the oceans.
- Sea level rise threatens coastal cities, low-lying islands, and millions of people living near coastlines.
- Even a small rise in sea level can cause devastating coastal flooding during storms.

### 9. Ocean Heat Content
- The **oceans act as a heat sink**, absorbing over 90% of the excess heat trapped by greenhouse gases.
- Measurements show that **ocean heat content has increased** significantly over the past decades.
- Warmer oceans lead to stronger tropical storms, altered marine ecosystems, and disrupted ocean currents.
- The increase in ocean heat content is one of the most **definitive indicators** that the Earth is gaining energy.

### 10. Temperature over Oceans
- Similar to land temperatures, **temperatures over ocean surfaces** have also been rising.
- This warming affects evaporation rates, precipitation patterns, and the distribution of marine life.
- Ocean temperatures influence weather patterns globally, including monsoon systems, which are critical for countries like India.

## 1.3 FACTORS AFFECTING GLOBAL WARMING (CAUSES)

The primary cause of global warming is the **emission of greenhouse gases** into the atmosphere due to human activities. The major sources include:

- **Carbon Dioxide (CO₂):** contributes about **80%**
- **Methane (CH₄) and Nitrous Oxide (N₂O):** contribute the remaining **20%**

### Factor 1: Carbon Dioxide (CO₂)

**Sources:**
- **Power Plants:** The burning of fossil fuels (coal, oil, natural gas) for electricity generation is the **single largest source** of CO₂ emissions. Power stations contribute about **21.3%** of total annual greenhouse gas emissions.
- **Transportation:** About **20% of CO₂** emitted into the atmosphere comes from burning gasoline and diesel in vehicle engines. Transportation fuels account for about **14%** of total emissions.
- **Industrial Processes:** Manufacturing industries release large amounts of CO₂ through combustion of fossil fuels and chemical reactions. Industrial processes contribute about **16.8%**.
- **Deforestation:** Trees absorb CO₂ through photosynthesis. When forests are cut and burned, the stored CO₂ is released into the atmosphere, and the carbon sink (forest) is also lost.

**Why is CO₂ so significant?**
- CO₂ remains in the atmosphere for **hundreds to thousands of years**.
- Its concentration has increased from about 280 ppm (pre-industrial) to over 420 ppm today — the highest in 800,000 years.

### Factor 2: Methane (CH₄)

**Key Facts:**
- Methane is **more than 20 times more effective than CO₂** at trapping heat in the atmosphere (over a 100-year period).
- This makes it an extremely potent greenhouse gas, even though it is present in smaller quantities.

**Sources:**
- **Rice Paddies:** Bacteria in flooded rice fields produce methane through anaerobic decomposition of organic matter.
- **Bovine Flatulence (Livestock):** Cattle, sheep, and other ruminants produce methane during digestion (enteric fermentation). Agricultural byproducts contribute about **12.5%** of emissions.
- **Bacteria in Bogs (Wetlands):** Natural wetlands release methane as organic matter decomposes without oxygen.
- **Fossil Fuel Manufacture and Distribution:** Methane leaks from coal mines, natural gas pipelines, and oil drilling operations. Fossil fuel retrieval accounts for about **11.3%** of emissions.
- **Landfills/Waste Disposal:** Decomposing organic waste in landfills produces methane. Waste disposal and treatment contributes about **3.4%**.

### Factor 3: Nitrous Oxide (N₂O)

**Key Facts:**
- Nitrous oxide is about **298 times more powerful** than CO₂ in terms of global warming potential over 100 years.
- It remains in the atmosphere for about 114 years.

**Sources:**
- **Agricultural Use of Fertilizers:** The use of nitrogen-based fertilizers in agriculture leads to the release of N₂O from soils through microbial processes — this is the **largest source**.
- **Nylon and Nitric Acid Production:** Industrial manufacturing of nylon and nitric acid releases N₂O as a byproduct.
- **Cars with Catalytic Converters:** Some types of catalytic converters in vehicles can produce N₂O as a byproduct of reducing other pollutants.
- **Burning of Organic Matter:** Burning of agricultural waste, biomass, and forests releases N₂O into the atmosphere.

### Factor 4: Deforestation

**What happens:**
- Forests are cut and burned for:
  - Residential purposes (urban expansion)
  - Industrialization
  - Agriculture (especially to create farmland)
  
**How it contributes to global warming:**
- **Direct emission of CO₂:** Burning forests releases the carbon stored in trees directly into the atmosphere.
- **Loss of carbon sinks:** Forests absorb CO₂ through photosynthesis. Deforestation reduces the Earth's capacity to absorb excess CO₂.
- **Albedo changes:** Dark forest soil absorbs more heat than the reflective forest canopy, further warming the area.
- Land use and biomass burning contribute about **10%** of total annual greenhouse gas emissions.

## 1.4 EFFECTS OF GLOBAL WARMING

Global warming has wide-ranging and serious consequences for the planet, its ecosystems, and human society. The major effects are:

1. **Frequent Temperature Extremes (Killer Heat Waves):**
   - As average temperatures rise, extreme heat events become more frequent, more intense, and last longer.
   - Heat waves cause heat strokes, dehydration, and death — especially among elderly, children, and outdoor workers.

2. **Changing Rainfall Patterns:**
   - Global warming alters the water cycle, causing some regions to receive more rainfall while others experience severe drought.
   - This disrupts agriculture, water supply, and natural ecosystems.

3. **Rise in Sea Levels:**
   - Melting glaciers and thermal expansion of ocean water cause sea levels to rise.
   - Low-lying coastal areas, islands, and delta regions are at risk of permanent inundation.

4. **Frequent Storms and Coastal Flooding:**
   - Warmer oceans provide more energy for tropical storms, hurricanes, and cyclones.
   - Coastal flooding becomes more frequent and severe as sea levels rise and storms intensify.

5. **Changes in Regional Climate Affecting Forests, Crop Yields, and Water Supplies:**
   - Climate zones shift, causing forests to die or migrate.
   - Crop yields decline in many regions due to heat stress, drought, and changing rainfall.
   - Rivers and lakes dependent on glacial meltwater may shrink or dry up seasonally.

6. **Drought:**
   - Many regions, especially subtropical areas, face prolonged and more severe droughts.
   - This leads to water scarcity for drinking, agriculture, and industry.

7. **Food Shortages Due to Shift in Agricultural Food Production:**
   - As climate conditions change, traditional agricultural zones shift.
   - Some areas can no longer grow their traditional crops.
   - Combined with population growth, this leads to food insecurity and famines.

8. **Greater Warming near the Poles:**
   - The Arctic and Antarctic regions are warming at **twice the global average rate**.
   - This is called **polar amplification** and leads to accelerated ice melting.

9. **Air Pollution Made Worse by Warming:**
   - Higher temperatures increase the formation of **ground-level ozone** (smog) and particulate matter.
   - This worsens air quality in cities.

10. **Asthma, Bronchitis, Emphysema Complications:**
    - Increased air pollution from warming leads to higher rates of respiratory diseases.
    - Higher pollen levels due to longer growing seasons worsen allergies and asthma.

11. **Expansion of Deserts into Existing Rangelands:**
    - Desertification increases as warming and drought conditions expand the boundaries of existing deserts into previously productive land.

12. **Unable to Contain Spread of Infectious Diseases:**
    - Warmer temperatures allow disease-carrying organisms (mosquitoes, ticks) to survive in new regions.
    - Diseases like malaria, dengue fever, and Lyme disease spread to areas that were previously too cold for them.

13. **Bleaching of Coral Reefs due to Acidification:**
    - CO₂ absorbed by oceans forms carbonic acid, lowering ocean pH (making oceans more acidic).
    - This **ocean acidification** causes coral bleaching — corals expel their symbiotic algae and turn white.
    - Coral bleaching eventually leads to coral death, destroying some of the world's most biodiverse ecosystems.

# PART 2: GLOBAL CLIMATE CHANGE

## 2.1 WHAT IS GLOBAL CLIMATE CHANGE?

**Climate change** is a long-term shift in global or regional climate patterns. It includes:
- Changes in temperature
- Changes in precipitation (rainfall, snowfall)
- Changes in wind patterns
- Changes in sea levels
- Changes in frequency and intensity of extreme weather events

> **Climate change is potentially one of the greatest threats to the environment, to biodiversity, and ultimately to the quality of human life.**

While global warming (rising temperatures) is the primary driver of current climate change, climate change itself is a **broader concept** that encompasses all the resulting changes in the Earth's climate systems.

---

## 2.2 INDICATORS OF CLIMATE CHANGE

The following 8 indicators clearly demonstrate that the Earth's climate is changing:

### 1. Global Warming
- The **increase in the average temperature** of the atmosphere and ocean, measured over the past 100 years.
- This is the **largest single indicator of climate change**.
- Some scientists believe the rate of temperature increase has **accelerated since the 1960s**.
- The 20th century was the warmest century in 1,000 years, and recent decades have broken temperature records repeatedly.

### 2. Changes in Polar and Glacial Ice
- **Large volumes of Earth's ice have been shrinking** in recent years.
- This is one of the most visible and dramatic indicators of climate change.

**Impacts of melting ice:**

**a. Rising Sea Levels:**
- Melting glaciers and ice sheets add massive amounts of water to the oceans.
- This causes sea levels to rise, threatening coastal regions worldwide.

**b. Habitat Loss for Animals:**
- **Polar bears** depend on large areas of sea ice to hunt their prey (primarily seals).
- Warmer temperatures melt the ice, making it difficult for polar bears to find food.
- Their population is declining as the hunting season on sea ice shortens.

**c. Cultural and Lifestyle Disruption (Inuit People):**
- The **Inuit population** (indigenous people of the Arctic) has a culture and lifestyle closely tied to the environment and wildlife.
- Their traditional lifestyle involves traveling and hunting **on sea ice**.
- The thinning and disappearing sea ice has made traditional practices **more difficult and unpredictable**, threatening their cultural survival.

### 3. Ocean Acidity
**What happens:**
- Oceans have absorbed about **half of all CO₂** produced from fossil fuel emissions over the past 200 years.
- When CO₂ dissolves in seawater, it forms **carbonic acid (H₂CO₃)**.
- This lowers the **pH of the ocean water**, making it more acidic — a process called **ocean acidification**.

**Effects of Ocean Acidity:**
- Threatens the ability of **corals and other organisms** (like oysters, clams, sea urchins) to build their calcium carbonate shells and hard skeletons.
- Affects the ability of **fish and plankton** to reproduce.
- Since plankton forms the base of the marine food web, disrupting plankton populations can have **devastating cascading effects** on entire marine ecosystems.
- Coral reefs — which support about 25% of all marine species — are particularly vulnerable.

### 4. Climate and Health
Climate is closely connected with human health. Rising temperatures and changing climate conditions affect health in the following ways:

**a. Risk of Disease and Infection:**
- Outbreaks of serious diseases tend to increase during periods of higher temperatures.
- **Malaria:** Mosquitoes that carry malaria thrive in warmer, wetter conditions and are expanding into new geographic areas.
- **Plague and other infectious diseases** show increased outbreaks in warmer periods.

**b. Increased Presence of Allergens:**
- Increasing temperatures affect:
  - The amount of **dust** in the air (desertification and droughts increase dust storms).
  - The growth of **mould** (fungal spores thrive in warm, humid conditions).
  - The release of **pollen** (plants produce more pollen over longer seasons in warmer conditions).
- All of these worsen **allergies, asthma, and other respiratory conditions**.

**c. Risk of Injury or Death due to Extreme Weather:**
- More frequent and intense extreme weather events (heat waves, floods, hurricanes, storms) directly cause injury and death.
- The **2003 European heat wave** alone claimed approximately **35,000 lives**.

### 5. Changing Wind Patterns
- Changes in the **distribution of heat** across the Earth's surface have led to changes in global wind patterns.
- **Speed, frequency, and direction** of winds over the Earth's surface have fluctuated unpredictably for several years.
- Some scientists believe that unusual wind patterns have **accelerated the melting of ice in the Arctic**.
- Changes in wind patterns also affect **precipitation distribution**, causing some areas to get more rain and others less.

### 6. Changing Precipitation Patterns
- **High temperatures increase the rate of evaporation** from land and water surfaces.
- When this warm, moisture-laden air rises and cools, it produces **rain or snow**.
- This alters the global precipitation (rainfall and snowfall) patterns:
  - Some regions receive **more precipitation** (heavier rains, floods).
  - Other regions receive **less** (droughts).
- **Example:** Since 1948, average annual temperatures across Ontario (Canada) have increased by as much as **1.4°C**. Annual precipitation and the total number of rainy days per year have increased. In Northern Ontario, there has been an increase in the **number of snowfalls and quantity** of snow.

### 7. Storm Intensity and Frequency
- The temperatures of **tropical ocean waters**, where tropical storms originate, have increased by **0.33°C since 1981**.
- Over the same period, the **strongest storms have become even stronger**.
- **Why?** Warmer seas hold more energy. This energy is available to be converted into hurricane-speed winds.
- The **intensity** (wind speed, rainfall) of tropical storms has increased significantly, even if the total number of storms has not dramatically changed.

### 8. Changing Biomes
- A **biome** is a large ecological community of plants and animals occupying a major habitat (e.g., tropical rainforest, grassland, tundra, desert).
- The plants and animals adapted to a particular biome **may no longer be able to survive** there if climate conditions change beyond their tolerance limits.
- As temperatures rise and rainfall patterns shift, biomes are **shifting poleward and to higher elevations**.
- Species that cannot migrate fast enough face **extinction**.
- **More than 1 million species** are threatened with extinction worldwide as a result of climate change.

---

## 2.3 EFFECT OF GLOBAL CLIMATE CHANGE ON HUMAN HEALTH

Climate change has direct and indirect impacts on human health:

- **Heat Waves:** Heat waves can lead to **heat stroke, dehydration, and death**. They are the **most common cause of weather-related deaths**.
  - **Example:** The 2003 summer heat wave in Europe claimed the lives of approximately **35,000 people**.

- **Allergies and Respiratory Health:** Climate change affects allergies and respiratory diseases through:
  - Increased air pollution (ground-level ozone, particulate matter)
  - Higher pollen counts and longer pollen seasons
  - Increased mould growth

- **Spread of Diseases through Food, Water, and Animals:** 
  - Warmer temperatures and altered rainfall patterns create favorable conditions for disease-causing organisms and their vectors.
  - Contamination of water supplies during floods spreads water-borne diseases.

- **WHO Statistics:** According to the **World Health Organization (WHO)**, climate change-related factors including malaria, diarrhea, malnutrition, and floods cause about **150,000 deaths worldwide annually**.

- **IPCC Data:** The **Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC)** reported that **250 million more Africans** are without potable (safe drinking) water due to climate-related stress on water resources.

- **Displacement of People:** Flooding linked to **rising sea levels** has displaced millions of people from their homes, creating **climate refugees** — people forced to migrate due to climate-related disasters.

# PART 3: ENVIRONMENTAL IMPACT ASSESSMENT (EIA)

---

## 3.1 INTRODUCTION TO EIA

### Definition of EIA:
**Environmental Impact Assessment (EIA)** can be defined as:
> *"The study to predict the effect of a proposed activity or project on the environment."*

In simpler terms, EIA is a **systematic process** of evaluating the potential environmental consequences of a proposed project **before it is implemented**, so that decision-makers can make informed choices.

### What EIA Does:
1. **Compares various alternatives** for a project and seeks to identify the one which represents the **best combination of economic and environmental costs and benefits**.

2. **Integrates environmental concerns** in developmental activities right at the time of initiating and preparing the **feasibility report** of the project. This early integration allows:
   - Environmental concerns to be addressed in the design stage itself
   - Mitigation measures to be built into the project plan from the beginning

3. **Prevents future liabilities or expensive alterations** in project design by identifying potential problems early when they are cheaper and easier to fix.

### Purpose of EIA:
- To ensure that **development decisions are made with full knowledge** of their likely environmental consequences.
- To provide **decision-makers, project developers, and the public** with information about the environmental implications of proposed projects.
- To ensure that **mitigation measures** are planned and implemented to minimize negative impacts.

---

## 3.2 THREE CORE VALUES OF EIA

EIA is guided by three fundamental principles:

### 1. Integrity
- The EIA process must be **fair, objective, unbiased, and balanced**.
- It should not be influenced by political pressures or economic interests.
- All relevant information, both positive and negative, should be presented honestly.

### 2. Utility
- The EIA process should provide **balanced, credible information for decision-making**.
- The EIA report should be useful and practical — it should help decision-makers understand the real impacts and make wise choices.
- Information should be presented in a clear, understandable manner.

### 3. Sustainability
- The EIA process should ultimately **result in Environmental Safeguard**.
- It should ensure that development does not compromise the ability of future generations to meet their needs.
- The goal is to find a balance between development and environmental protection.

---

## 3.3 BENEFITS OF ENVIRONMENTAL ASSESSMENT

EIA offers numerous benefits:

1. **Protection of Environment:** By identifying potential environmental impacts before a project begins, EIA helps prevent irreversible environmental damage.

2. **Optimum Utilization of Resources:** EIA helps ensure that natural resources are used efficiently and sustainably, avoiding wasteful use.

3. **Saves Overall Time and Cost of the Project:** By identifying problems early in the planning stage, EIA prevents costly modifications and delays during construction or operation.

4. **Promotes Community Participation:** EIA processes involve public hearings and consultations, giving local communities a voice in decisions that affect their environment and lives.

5. **Informs Decision Makers:** EIA provides government authorities, project developers, and other decision-makers with comprehensive, scientifically sound information.

6. **Lays Base for Environmentally Sound Projects:** EIA helps design projects that are both economically viable and environmentally responsible.

---

## 3.4 EVOLUTION AND HISTORY OF EIA

### Origin:
- **Environmental Impact Assessment** started as a mandatory regulatory procedure.
- It **originated in the early 1970s** with the implementation of the **National Environmental Policy Act (NEPA) 1969** in the **United States**.
- NEPA was the first law to require federal agencies in the USA to consider environmental impacts before making major decisions.

### Spread to Other Countries:
- **EIA took off after the mid-1980s**, after the **World Bank adopted EIA** as a requirement for major development projects it funded.
- Countries that borrowed money from the World Bank (borrower countries) were required to **undertake EIA under the Bank's supervision**.
- This requirement spread the practice of EIA globally.

### Current Status:
- Today, **EIA is a formal, legally mandated process in more than 100 countries** around the world.
- Each country has developed its own EIA framework based on its specific needs, legal systems, and environmental conditions.

### EIA in India:
- **Environmental Clearance from the central government is required for 32 categories of development projects** under industrial sectors.
- These include:
  - **Mining** (coal, iron ore, minerals, etc.)
  - **Thermal power plants** (coal-based, gas-based)
  - **River valley projects** (dams, irrigation projects, hydro-power)
  - **Infrastructure** (roads, highways, ports, harbours, airports)

---

## 3.5 EIA — CYCLE AND PROCEDURE IN INDIA

The **EIA process in India** consists of the following 9 phases:

1. **Screening**
2. **Scoping and Consideration of Alternatives**
3. **Baseline Data Collection**
4. **Impact Analysis**
5. **Mitigation and Environmental Impact Statement**
6. **Public Hearing**
7. **Environmental Management Plan**
8. **Decision Making**
9. **Monitoring the Clearance Condition**

Let us understand each phase in detail:

---

### PHASE 1: SCREENING

**Definition:**
Screening is the **first stage of EIA**. It determines:
- **Whether** the proposed project requires an EIA at all.
- **If yes, what level** of assessment is required (comprehensive or rapid).

**Screening Criteria:**
The level of review required is determined based on:
- **Scale of Investment** — How large is the project financially?
- **Type of Development** — What kind of project is it? (industrial, infrastructure, mining, etc.)
- **Location of Development** — Where is the project located? (near sensitive ecosystems, populated areas, water bodies, etc.)

Based on screening, projects are classified into three categories:

#### Project Category 'A':
- These projects **typically require a full, comprehensive EIA**.
- The designation is based on **project type, scale, and location**.
- Potentially significant environmental issues for these projects may lead to:
  - **Changes in land use** (agricultural land converted to industrial)
  - **Changes to social environment** (displacement of people)
  - **Changes to physical environment** (water bodies, landforms)
  - **Changes to biological environment** (forests, wildlife habitats)
- **Examples:** Large thermal power plants, large hydroelectric dams, large mining projects, major ports.

#### Project Category 'B':
- The **only difference** from Category 'A' is the **scale** of the project.
- **Larger power plants** fall under Category 'A'; **Medium-sized power plants** fall under Category 'B'.
- These projects are generally **not located in environmentally sensitive areas**.
- **Mitigation measures** for Category 'B' projects are **more easily prescribed** because the potential impacts are less complex.

#### Project Category 'C':
- This category is for projects that **typically DO NOT require an environmental assessment**.
- These projects are **unlikely to have significant adverse environmental impacts**.
- Examples include small-scale, low-risk projects in non-sensitive locations.

---

### PHASE 2: SCOPING

**Definition:**
Scoping is the stage that **identifies key issues and significant impacts** that should be further investigated in the EIA study. It also **defines the boundary and time limit** of the study.

**Who does scoping?**
- Done by a **consultant** in consultation with the **project proponent** and with guidance from the regulatory **agency**.

**What is assessed during scoping?**
- **Quantifiable impacts** are assessed based on:
  - **Magnitude** — How large is the potential impact?
  - **Prevalence** — Over how wide an area does the impact occur?
  - **Frequency** — How often does the impact occur?
  - **Duration** — How long does the impact last?
  
- **Non-quantifiable impacts** (like aesthetic value, recreational value, cultural significance) are also considered.
- **Significance** of impacts is usually determined through **socio-economic criteria**.

**After Scoping:**
- Areas where the project could have significant impact are identified.
- The **Baseline status** of these areas is monitored.
- **Likely changes** in these areas due to construction and operation of the proposed project are predicted.

---

### PHASE 3: BASELINE DATA COLLECTION

**Definition:**
Baseline data refers to information about the **current state of the environment** before the project begins. It serves as a reference point against which future changes can be measured.

**Why is Baseline Data Important?**
- **Impact prediction** is a way of "mapping" the **environmental consequences** of the significant aspects of the project and its alternatives.
- Environmental impact can never be predicted with **absolute certainty** — but collecting baseline data helps **reduce the degree of uncertainty**.
- By understanding the current conditions, we can better predict how they will change after the project is implemented.

**Categories of Baseline Data to be Collected:**

#### AIR:
- Changes in the **ambient level** and **ground level concentrations** of pollutants due to emissions from:
  - **Point sources** (e.g., a single smokestack)
  - **Line sources** (e.g., a road with traffic emissions)
  - **Area sources** (e.g., a cluster of small industries)
- Effects on **soils, materials, vegetation, and human health** due to air pollution.

#### NOISE:
- Changes in the **ambient noise level** due to noise generated from:
  - Equipment and machinery used in the project
  - Movement of vehicles during construction and operation
- Effects of increased noise on **fauna (animals) and human health**.

#### WATER:
- **Availability** to competing users — will the project reduce water available to others?
- **Changes in water quality** — will the project pollute surface water or groundwater?
- **Sediment transport** — will the project alter how sediment moves through water bodies?
- **Ingress of saline water** — will the project allow saltwater to intrude into freshwater systems?

#### LAND:
- **Changes in land-use and drainage patterns** — how will the project alter the way land is used?
- **Changes in land quality** including effects of waste disposal — will the project contaminate soil?
- **Changes in shoreline/riverbank and their stability** — will the project cause erosion or bank collapse?

#### BIOLOGICAL:
- **Deforestation and shrinkage of animal habitat** — how much forest will be cleared?
- **Impact on flora and fauna** due to contaminants and pollutants from the project.
- **Impact on rare and endangered species**, endemic species, and migratory paths of animals including birds.
- **Impact on breeding and nesting grounds** of wildlife.

#### SOCIO-ECONOMIC:
- **Impact on the local community** including demographic changes (population shifts, migration).
- **Impact on economic status** of the local population (loss of livelihoods, economic opportunities).
- **Impact on human health** — will the project cause health problems for nearby communities?

---

### PHASE 4: IMPACT ANALYSIS

**This phase involves detailed prediction and evaluation of the identified significant impacts.**

The impacts are analyzed across all environmental components (air, water, land, biological, socio-economic) using appropriate scientific methods and models.

---

### PHASE 5: ASSESSMENT OF ALTERNATIVES, MITIGATION MEASURES, AND EIA REPORT

#### Assessment of Alternatives:
- For every project, **possible alternatives must be identified** and their **environmental attributes compared**.
- Alternatives considered include:
  - **Alternative locations** for the project
  - **Alternative process technologies** (cleaner, less polluting methods)
  - **"No project" alternative** — what happens if the project is not built at all? This must always be considered.
- Based on the best environmental option that also provides **optimum economic benefits** to the community, alternatives are **ranked** and the best one is selected.

#### Delineation of Mitigation Measures:
- A **mitigation plan** for the selected project option must be drawn up.
- Mitigation measures are actions taken to:
  - **Prevent** or reduce anticipated negative impacts
  - **Compensate** for impacts that cannot be avoided
- The mitigation plan is supplemented with an **Environmental Management Plan (EMP)** to guide environmental improvement.

#### Environmental Impact Assessment (EIA) Report:
- The **EIA Report** provides the decision-maker with comprehensive information on different environmental scenarios:
  - **"With the project"** scenario — what the environment will look like if the project goes ahead.
  - **"Without the project"** scenario — what the environment would look like if the project does not proceed.
  - **"With project alternatives"** — comparisons of different project options.
  - **Uncertainties** should also be reflected in the EIA report — where predictions are uncertain, this should be honestly stated.

---

### PHASE 6: PUBLIC HEARING

**Definition:**
After the EIA report is prepared and made public, the **affected public must be informed and consulted** on the proposed development.

**Who gets the EIA Report Summary?**
The summary of the EIA report must be provided to all people affected by the proposed project, including:
- **Bona fide local residents** (people who live in the area affected by the project)
- **Local associations** (community organizations, resident welfare associations)
- **Environmental groups active in the area**
- **Any other person located at the project site or sites of displacement** (people who may be relocated due to the project)

**Purpose of Public Hearing:**
- To ensure **transparency and democratic participation** in environmental decision-making.
- To allow affected communities to raise concerns and objections.
- To incorporate local knowledge and community concerns into the EIA process.
- Public hearing after the EIA report is made public is mandatory in India under EIA Notification 2006.

---

### PHASE 7: ENVIRONMENTAL MANAGEMENT PLAN (EMP)

**Definition:**
The Environmental Management Plan (EMP) is a comprehensive plan that details **how the environmental impacts identified in the EIA will be managed** throughout the project lifecycle.

**EMP is Critical for:**
- Monitoring the **clearance conditions** imposed by the regulatory authority.
- Ensuring that all **commitments made** in the EIA report are actually implemented.

**Components of EMP:**

1. **Delineation of Mitigation Measures:**
   - Includes **prevention and control measures** for each environmental component (air, water, land, biology, socio-economy).
   - Includes **rehabilitation and resettlement plans** for affected populations.

2. **Delineation of Monitoring Scheme:**
   - A detailed **monitoring plan** for compliance with clearance conditions.
   - Specifies what environmental parameters will be monitored, how often, and using what methods.

3. **Delineation of Implementation Plan:**
   - Includes **scheduling** of all mitigation and monitoring activities.
   - Includes **resource allocations** — financial, human, and technical resources needed.

---

### PHASE 8: DECISION MAKING

**Process:**
- **Consultation** between the:
  - **Project proponent** (the company/organization proposing the project), assisted by a consultant
  - **Impact assessment authority** (the regulatory body, e.g., Ministry of Environment, Forest and Climate Change in India), assisted by an expert group if necessary.

**How is the Decision Made?**
- The decision on **environmental clearance** is arrived at through several steps, including:
  - **Evaluation of the EIA report**
  - **Evaluation of the Environmental Management Plan (EMP)**
  - Review of public hearing comments
  - Expert committee assessment

**Possible Outcomes:**
- **Approved:** The project receives environmental clearance (possibly with conditions).
- **Not Approved:** The project is rejected due to unacceptable environmental impacts.
- **Redesign Required:** The project must be modified and resubmitted.
- **Resubmit:** Additional information or studies are required.

---

### PHASE 9: MONITORING THE CLEARANCE CONDITIONS

**Purpose:**
After the project receives environmental clearance and begins, monitoring ensures that the commitments made in the EIA report are actually being kept.

**Key Points:**
- **Monitoring must be done during BOTH:**
  - **Construction phase** (monitoring impacts during building of the project)
  - **Operation phase** (monitoring impacts during normal functioning of the project)

- **Why Monitor?**
  - To ensure that the **commitments made** in the EIA report are compiled with.
  - To verify that the **predictions made** in the EIA report are correct.
  - If predictions were wrong (e.g., pollution is higher than predicted), corrective action can be taken.

- **Corrective Actions:**
  - If impacts **exceed the predicted levels** or violate clearance conditions, **corrective actions must be taken**.
  - This may include upgrading pollution control equipment, changing operating procedures, or additional mitigation measures.

## 3.7 COMPONENTS OF EIA (EIA REPORT CONTENTS)

### Comprehensive EIA vs. Rapid EIA:
Both types vary only in the **time scale of data collected**:

- **Rapid EIA:**
  - Used for a **speedier appraisal process**.
  - Uses **"one season" data** (data collected for only one season, typically the most stressful season for the project type).
  - Acceptable **only if it does not compromise the quality of decision-making**.
  - Used when time is a constraint.

- **Comprehensive EIA:**
  - Uses data collected over **all seasons (typically one full year or more)**.
  - Provides a more complete picture of environmental conditions.
  - Required for projects with potentially major and complex impacts.

### Components of the EIA Report:

Depending on the **nature, location, and scale** of the project, the EIA report should contain all or some of the following components:

---

### A. AIR ENVIRONMENT

The air environment assessment includes:

1. **Determination of the impact zone** and developing a monitoring network (typically 7-10 km from the periphery of the project site).

2. **Monitoring the existing status of ambient air quality** within the impacted region — measuring current levels of pollutants (SO₂, NOₓ, PM₁₀, PM₂.₅, CO, etc.).

3. **Monitoring site-specific meteorological data:**
   - Wind speed and wind direction
   - Humidity
   - Ambient temperature
   - Environmental lapse rate (how temperature changes with altitude)

4. **Estimation of qualities (quantity and composition) of air emissions** from the proposed project site — what pollutants will be emitted and in what quantities?

5. **Identification, quantification, and evaluation of other potential emissions** within the impact zone from other existing sources.

6. **Prediction of changes in ambient air quality** through air quality dispersion modeling — using mathematical models to predict how pollutants will spread and concentrate.

7. **Evaluation of the adequacy of proposed pollution control devices** to meet gaseous emission standards and ambient air quality standards set by the regulatory authority.

8. **Delineation of mitigation measures** at:
   - **Source** (reducing emissions at the point of generation)
   - **Pathways** (preventing pollutants from reaching receptors)
   - **Receptors** (protecting people and ecosystems from pollutants)

---

### B. NOISE ENVIRONMENT

The noise environment assessment includes:

1. **Monitoring the present status (baseline) of noise levels** within the impact zone — measuring existing noise levels at various locations.

2. **Predicting the future noise levels** that will result from the proposed project, including:
   - Noise from machinery and equipment
   - Noise from vehicular movement (transportation of materials, workers, products)

3. **Identification of impacts** due to any anticipated rise in noise levels on the surrounding environment:
   - Effects on human health (hearing damage, stress, sleep disturbance)
   - Effects on wildlife (disruption of communication, breeding, migration)

4. **Recommendations on mitigation measures** for noise pollution:
   - Sound barriers/walls
   - Noise dampening equipment
   - Operational time restrictions
   - Vegetation buffers

---

### C. WATER ENVIRONMENT

The water environment assessment includes:

1. **Study of existing ground and surface water resources** with respect to:
   - **Quality** — What is the current water quality? (chemical, physical, biological parameters)
   - **Quantity** — How much water is available in the region?
   - Assessment is done within the **impact zone** of the proposed project.

2. **Prediction of the impact on water resources** due to:
   - Pumping/extraction of water for the project
   - Changes in drainage patterns
   - Contamination from project activities

3. **Quantification and characterization of wastewater** generated by the proposed activity:
   - Volume of wastewater
   - Composition (toxic substances, organic matter, suspended solids)

4. **Evaluation of the proposed pollution prevention and wastewater treatment system** — is the proposed treatment adequate to meet standards?

5. **Prediction of impacts of effluent discharge** on the quality of the receiving water body (river, lake, sea) using appropriate hydrological and water quality models.

6. **Assessment of the feasibility of water recycling and reuse** — can wastewater be treated and reused within the project, reducing both water consumption and discharge?

---

### D. BIOLOGICAL ENVIRONMENT

The biological environment assessment includes:

1. **Survey of flora (plants) and fauna (animals)** in the project area, clearly delineating the **season and duration** of the survey (since species distributions change seasonally).

2. **Assessment of the flora and fauna** within the impact zone of the project — cataloguing what species are present and their abundance.

3. **Assessment of potential damage to terrestrial and aquatic flora and fauna** due to:
   - Discharge of effluents (liquid waste)
   - Gaseous emissions from the project

4. **Assessment of damage to terrestrial flora and fauna** due to:
   - **Air pollution** (e.g., acid deposition damaging plants)
   - **Land-use changes** (e.g., forest clearance)
   - **Landscape changes**

5. **Assessment of damage to aquatic and marine flora and fauna** due to:
   - **Physical disturbances** (e.g., dredging, damming)
   - **Alternations in water quality** (temperature, chemical composition)

6. **Prediction of biological stresses** (cumulative effects on ecosystems) within the impact zone of the proposed project.

7. **Delineation of mitigation measures** to prevent and/or reduce biological damage:
   - Wildlife corridors
   - Compensatory afforestation
   - Restrictions on sensitive area operations
   - Protected area buffers

---

### E. LAND ENVIRONMENT

The land environment assessment includes:

1. **Studies on:**
   - **Soil characteristics** — soil type, fertility, permeability, stability
   - **Existing land use** — how is the land currently being used? (agriculture, forest, residential, industrial)
   - **Topography** — the shape and elevation of the land
   - **Landscape** — the visual character of the area
   - **Drainage patterns** — how water flows across and through the land
   - All assessed within the impact zone.

2. **Estimation of impacts of the project** on:
   - Land use (will agricultural land be converted?)
   - Landscape (will the visual character change dramatically?)
   - Topography (will landforms be altered?)
   - Drainage (will natural water flow be disrupted?)
   - Hydrology (will groundwater levels change?)

3. **Identification of potential utility of treated effluent in land application** — can treated wastewater be beneficially used for irrigation? What are the subsequent impacts on soil and crops?

4. **Estimation and characterization of solid wastes** generated by the project and **delineation of management options** for:
   - Minimization of waste generation
   - Environmentally compatible disposal methods (landfilling, incineration, recycling)

---

### F. SOCIO-ECONOMIC AND HEALTH ENVIRONMENT

The socio-economic and health environment assessment includes:

1. **Collection of demographic and related socio-economic data:**
   - Population size, composition, growth rate
   - Employment patterns
   - Income levels
   - Education and social infrastructure

2. **Collection of epidemiological data** including studies on:
   - **Prominent endemic diseases** — what diseases are already present in the area?
   - **Morbidity rates** (rates of disease) among the population within the impact zone.

3. **Projection of anticipated changes in socio-economic and health status** due to the project and its related activities:
   - Changes in employment
   - Changes in income
   - Changes in population composition
   - Changes in health outcomes

4. **Delineation of measures to minimize adverse impacts:**
   - Healthcare facilities
   - Livelihood restoration programs
   - Community development initiatives

5. **Assessment of impact on significant historical, cultural, and archaeological sites** in the area — will the project damage heritage sites?

6. **Assessment of economic benefits** arising from the project:
   - Employment generation
   - Revenue to government
   - Development of infrastructure
   - Improvement in living standards

7. **Assessment of rehabilitation requirements** with special emphasis on **scheduled areas** (areas with scheduled castes and tribes who have special legal protections in India).

---

### G. RISK ASSESSMENT

The Risk Assessment component includes:

1. **Hazard Identification** using:
   - Hazard indices
   - **Inventory analysis** (what hazardous materials are present?)
   - **Dam breaking probability** (for dam projects — likelihood and consequences of dam failure)
   - **Natural hazard probability** (earthquakes, landslides, floods)

2. **Maximum Credible Accidents (MCA) Analysis:**
   - Identifies and analyzes the **worst-case accident scenarios** that could plausibly occur.
   - Determines the potential impact of such accidents.

3. **Consequences Analysis of failures and accidents:**
   - Fire (e.g., petroleum storage tank fire)
   - **Explosion** (e.g., gas pipeline explosion)
   - **Hazardous releases** (e.g., toxic chemical spills)
   - **Dam breaks** (catastrophic failure of water retention structures)

4. **Hazard and Operability (HAZOP) Studies:**
   - Systematic examination of the project's operational processes to identify potential hazards.

5. **Assessment of risks** based on the above evaluations — ranking risks by probability and severity.

6. **Preparation of an on-site and off-site Disaster Management Plan (DMP):**
   - **On-site plan:** How will the project's own personnel and infrastructure respond to a disaster?
   - **Off-site plan:** How will external agencies (police, fire, civil administration, hospitals) respond to protect the surrounding community?

---

### H. ENVIRONMENT MANAGEMENT PLAN (EMP)

(As detailed under Phase 7 above)

1. **Delineation of mitigation measures** including:
   - Prevention and control for each environmental component
   - Rehabilitation and resettlement plan for affected communities

2. **Delineation of monitoring scheme** for compliance with clearance conditions.

3. **Delineation of implementation plan** including:
   - Scheduling of all activities
   - Resource allocations (financial, human, technical)

## 3.8 EIA — BENEFITS AND FLAWS

### BENEFITS of EIA:

| Benefit | Explanation |
|---------|-------------|
| **Provides systematic method of impact assessment** | EIA provides a structured, scientific approach to evaluating environmental impacts, ensuring nothing is overlooked. |
| **Estimates cost/benefit trade-off of alternative actions** | EIA compares different project options, helping identify which option gives the best balance between economic benefits and environmental costs. |
| **Facilitates public participation** | EIA processes include public hearings, giving communities a voice in decisions affecting their environment. |
| **Provides effective mechanism for coordination** | EIA brings together different departments, agencies, and stakeholders. |
| **Environmental integration** | EIA integrates environmental considerations into the development planning process from the start. |
| **Negotiations** | EIA provides a platform for negotiating between developers and affected communities. |
| **Feed back** | Information from monitoring contributes to improving future projects. |
| **Top-level decision making** | EIA informs high-level government decisions about project approvals. |
| **Achieves balance between development and environmental concern** | EIA is the primary tool for ensuring that development is environmentally responsible. |

### FLAWS of EIA:

| Flaw | Explanation |
|------|-------------|
| **Time consuming** | The EIA process can take months or years, which may delay urgently needed projects. |
| **Costly** | Conducting a comprehensive EIA requires significant financial resources for surveys, studies, consultations, and report preparation. |
| **Little public participation in actual implementation** | While public hearings are held, communities often have little say in the actual decision or how the project is implemented. |
| **Unavailability of reliable data (mostly in developing countries)** | EIA depends on good baseline data, which is often lacking in developing countries due to poor monitoring infrastructure. |
| **Too focused on scientific analysis (sometime)** | EIA can sometimes over-emphasize quantitative scientific data at the expense of qualitative concerns like cultural values and community perceptions. |
| **Compliance monitoring after EIA is seldom carried out** | Even when environmental clearances are given with conditions, monitoring of whether these conditions are met is often inadequate. |
